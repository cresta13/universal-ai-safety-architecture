
# Open Research Problems

The entries below remain open; candidate mechanisms are not guarantees.

## Formal Reachable Future Authority tractability

1. **Why it matters:** hidden future power defeats gating.
2. **What is already known:** typed authority graphs and bounded witness search are available.
3. **What remains unknown:** sound/complete bounds under identity creation, secrets and adaptation.
4. **Candidate research directions:** symbolic search, abstract interpretation, attack graphs and empirical benchmarks.
5. **Prototype now:** cloud IAM graph prototype.
6. **Evidence of progress:** published benchmark recall/bounds and failure characterization.
7. **Failure modes:** State-space explosion can defeat analysis; omitted edge semantics or invalid pruning can make the result unsound; apparent tractability may hold only on toy graphs.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AuthorityVisibility/TransitiveAuthorityVisibility, Authority Graph, AuthorityImpactProtocol.

## Authority Expansion Gradient

1. **Why it matters:** rapid expansion matters before total power is large.
2. **What is already known:** ΔA and observable transition concepts exist.
3. **What remains unknown:** stable gradient across heterogeneous authority dimensions.
4. **Candidate research directions:** vector change calculus and event forecasting.
5. **Prototype now:** IAM/update time-series detector.
6. **Evidence of progress:** predictive calibration on held-out expansion events.
7. **Failure modes:** The metric may change under an equivalent graph representation, benign configuration churn may create a false trend, and heterogeneous authority dimensions may resist valid comparison.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** RFA, AuthorityExpansionSensitivity, Monitoring.

## Coalition Reachable Authority

1. **Why it matters:** agents can combine powers absent individually.
2. **What is already known:** CRA/CPE require composition beyond union.
3. **What remains unknown:** coalition discovery and combinatorial tractability.
4. **Candidate research directions:** hypergraphs, game theory, causal composition and simulation.
5. **Prototype now:** fleet/IAM coalition scenarios.
6. **Evidence of progress:** hidden-coalition benchmark and bounded miss rates.
7. **Failure modes:** Coalition search may explode combinatorially; hidden coordination channels may be absent from the graph; emergent capability may not reduce to the union of member capabilities.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** RFA, CPE, CoalitionAuthorityCoverage.

## Unknown capability discovery

1. **Why it matters:** new combinations escape existing tests.
2. **What is already known:** Novel Capability Gate and provisional class bound deployment.
3. **What remains unknown:** coverage of genuinely novel capability.
4. **Candidate research directions:** open-ended evals, behavioral novelty detection, tool-composition fuzzing.
5. **Prototype now:** sealed tool-use challenges.
6. **Evidence of progress:** replicable discovery before field incident.
7. **Failure modes:** Benchmark leakage can simulate discovery, a novelty proxy can be mistaken for harmful capability, and the method may fail on genuinely out-of-distribution mechanisms.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** Capability Management, UnknownHazardSearchCapability.

## Human Harm Reachability completeness

1. **Why it matters:** mediated and nonphysical harms are easy to omit.
2. **What is already known:** causal Human Harm Paths can be recorded/tested.
3. **What remains unknown:** how complete a path catalogue can be.
4. **Candidate research directions:** causal modeling, participatory hazard analysis and incident mining.
5. **Prototype now:** smart-speaker HHR corpus.
6. **Evidence of progress:** new path recall across populations and modalities.
7. **Failure modes:** Models may omit mediated human behavior, leave causal links incomplete, or fail to bound social and environmental paths that carry harm.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AHCD, HumanHarmReachabilityCoverage.

## Guardian reasoning verification

1. **Why it matters:** evidence integrity does not prove forecast truth.
2. **What is already known:** ECA/SEP/DEV bound mechanically checkable claims.
3. **What remains unknown:** validation of world models and pruning.
4. **Candidate research directions:** challenge suites, calibration, proof-carrying subclaims, diversity.
5. **Prototype now:** ECA/DEV lab.
6. **Evidence of progress:** lower miss/calibration error under adaptive tests.
7. **Failure modes:** A verifier may validate a derivation built on false premises; model and verifier errors may be correlated; learned assumptions about the world may remain unverifiable.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** CAG, CounterfactualSearchQuality, EvidenceVerifiability.

## Automation bias

1. **Why it matters:** persuasive safety automation can distort human legitimacy.
2. **What is already known:** uncertainty-first interfaces and non-alert audits are specified.
3. **What remains unknown:** robust decision behavior under time pressure.
4. **Candidate research directions:** human factors experiments and institutional controls.
5. **Prototype now:** simulated Guardian decisions.
6. **Evidence of progress:** reduced inappropriate reliance without unsafe override.
7. **Failure modes:** People may defer to a persuasive Evidence-Carrying Alert despite weak evidence, alert fatigue may suppress scrutiny, or nominal approval may occur without meaningful comprehension.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** CAG, HSC.

## Machine-speed legitimacy

1. **Why it matters:** per-action human clicks do not scale.
2. **What is already known:** humans may authorize envelopes and floors.
3. **What remains unknown:** safe envelope amendment under rapid novelty.
4. **Candidate research directions:** mechanism design, threshold policy and formal delegation.
5. **Prototype now:** bounded trading/robot envelope.
6. **Evidence of progress:** low unsafe expansion and acceptable operational latency.
7. **Failure modes:** Human or governance latency may be incompatible with operations, a preauthorized envelope may become too broad, or the legitimacy service may become a bottleneck and common root of failure.

## Offline replication budgets

1. **Why it matters:** offline identities/counters can be cloned or double-spent.
2. **What is already known:** signed lineage and bounded tokens are candidates.
3. **What remains unknown:** enforcement without trusted connectivity/roots.
4. **Candidate research directions:** secure hardware, physical inventory and reconciliation protocols.
5. **Prototype now:** ReplicationAuthorizationProtocol with monotonic counters.
6. **Evidence of progress:** documented bounds under clone/root compromise tests.
7. **Failure modes:** Tokens may be cloned, counters rolled back, state forked, revocation delayed, or reconciliation may accept the same authorization more than once.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** ReplicationAuthorizationProtocol, ReplicationControlStrength.

## User-report manipulation and Sybil resistance

1. **Why it matters:** user/operator reports are essential evidence inputs, but raw report volume must not become a popularity vote or an attack channel.
2. **What is already known:** deduplication, reporter provenance, validation state, and Sybil/spam controls can be required before a report affects trustworthiness metrics.
3. **What remains unknown:** how to preserve legitimate low-friction reporting while detecting coordinated false reports, retaliatory mass reporting, provider suppression, and subtle evidence poisoning.
4. **Candidate research directions:** graph-based occurrence clustering, privacy-preserving reporter reputation, cross-source corroboration models, adversarial queue-load exercises, and fairness tests for underrepresented reporters.
5. **Prototype now:** an append-only AI Error Report intake with pseudonymous reporter receipts, occurrence deduplication, Sybil flags, validation sampling, and reporter-visible disposition.
6. **Evidence of progress:** seeded Sybil campaigns are detected without suppressing genuine minority reports; false duplicate grouping is caught; validation backlog stays bounded under attack.
7. **Failure modes:** anti-Sybil rules may silence legitimate clusters, expose reporter identity, or let providers label inconvenient reports as spam.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AIErrorReportingProtocol, AIErrorRegistry, UserErrorReportingChannel, ConfirmedAIError, UnresolvedMaterialReportExposure.



## Sparse critical-error estimation

1. **Why it matters:** critical errors may be rare enough that ordinary averages create unjustified confidence, especially when the consequence class is high.
2. **What is already known:** zero observed events require an upper bound, and critical errors remain hard caps rather than values in a compensating average.
3. **What remains unknown:** how much evidence is enough for active ratings in domains where true critical-event rates are below feasible test observation.
4. **Candidate research directions:** rare-event statistics, stress-stratified exposure accounting, near-miss incorporation, Bayesian sensitivity analysis with conservative priors, and domain-specific minimum-evidence floors.
5. **Prototype now:** a critical-error calculator that reports exact upper bounds for zero observations, near-miss counts, unresolved credible reports, and evidence volume needed for each rating level.
6. **Evidence of progress:** independent teams reach similar bounds on shared fixtures; decision makers correctly interpret zero-observation cases as bounded uncertainty rather than proof of safety.
7. **Failure modes:** sparse data may force too many profiles into Unrated/Provisional states, while optimistic priors may let unsupported Active ratings through.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** CriticalErrorRate, TrustworthinessScoringPolicy, TrustworthinessStatus, EvidenceSupportedRating, CriticalErrorCap.



## Trustworthiness carryover across model updates

1. **Why it matters:** old evidence can become misleading after model, tool, policy, retrieval, or configuration changes, but requiring full reset after every small update may be impractical.
2. **What is already known:** carryover must be versioned, scoped, and capped by Capability Delta, Authority Delta, Hazard Delta, and domain-specific change rules.
3. **What remains unknown:** which behavioral, architectural, and operational deltas reliably predict that prior evidence remains valid for each domain.
4. **Candidate research directions:** delta-sensitive regression suites, capability-drift probes, evidence-decay models, update lineage analysis, and falsification studies of proposed carryover rules.
5. **Prototype now:** a Trustworthiness Carryover Rule engine that links update metadata to allowed evidence reuse, required revalidation, and ChangeCarryoverCap.
6. **Evidence of progress:** the engine blocks version-laundering fixtures, preserves valid narrow evidence across minor patches, and explains why each evidence item carried over or expired.
7. **Failure modes:** providers may split material changes into small updates, hide tool/policy changes, or overfit carryover tests while behavior changes in production.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** TrustworthinessCarryoverRule, ChangeCarryoverCap, CapabilityDelta, AuthorityDelta, HazardDelta, UpdateSafetyProtocol.



## Open-ended qualitative evaluation

1. **Why it matters:** many consequential AI tasks are open-ended, so correctness cannot always be reduced to one known answer or deterministic benchmark.
2. **What is already known:** registered rubrics, explicit verdicts, evidence references, independent adjudication, and preserved disagreement improve qualitative evaluation discipline.
3. **What remains unknown:** how to make qualitative verdicts reproducible enough for ratings without pretending that subjective or contextual judgments are objective ground truth.
4. **Candidate research directions:** rubric reliability studies, adversarial ambiguity sets, expert/AI adjudication comparisons, confidence calibration for qualitative verdicts, and minority-report preservation methods.
5. **Prototype now:** an AI Evaluation Rubric workbench with blinded qualitative cases, paired evaluators, adjudication trails, and explicit Indeterminate outcomes.
6. **Evidence of progress:** inter-evaluator disagreement falls on stable rubrics; high-consequence disagreements are escalated rather than averaged; qualitative verdicts survive independent replication.
7. **Failure modes:** rubrics may encode institutional bias, reward polished but unsafe answers, or hide irreducible uncertainty behind confident narrative judgments.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AIEvaluationRubric, AIEvaluationVerdict, IndependentAIEvaluator, EvaluatorDisagreement, RatingExplanationRecord.



## Operational exposure denominators

1. **Why it matters:** error rates are meaningless if the denominator mixes easy and hard tasks, excludes failures, or counts events that were never real opportunities for error.
2. **What is already known:** Operational Exposure Units must be domain-defined, versioned, stratified, and tied to configuration and task class.
3. **What remains unknown:** how to define exposure units for interactive, multi-step, delegated, or partially observable workflows without creating incentives to reshape usage accounting.
4. **Candidate research directions:** denominator ontologies, workflow segmentation, privacy-preserving telemetry audits, missing-exposure estimation, and adversarial denominator-manipulation fixtures.
5. **Prototype now:** an exposure ledger that labels eligible/ineligible events, task strata, configuration, context, missing logs, and exclusion reasons.
6. **Evidence of progress:** independent auditors reproduce denominator counts; hidden failed contexts are detected; rates remain stable under legitimate logging pipeline changes.
7. **Failure modes:** providers may inflate exposure with low-risk events, exclude hard edge cases as ineligible, or use privacy constraints to hide denominator gaps.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** OperationalExposureUnit, OperationalEvidenceVolume, ConfirmedErrorRate, TrustworthinessScoringPolicy, AITrustworthinessRegistry.



## Correlated AI evaluators

1. **Why it matters:** multiple AI evaluators can appear independent while sharing training data, model family, provider incentives, infrastructure, or failure modes.
2. **What is already known:** Evaluator Independence Profiles and disagreement preservation can expose some common-mode risk.
3. **What remains unknown:** how to measure hidden evaluator correlation and how much diversity is enough for high-consequence evidence claims.
4. **Candidate research directions:** evaluator provenance graphs, common-blind-spot challenge sets, ensemble dependency analysis, organization/infrastructure correlation metrics, and post-incident evaluator-failure studies.
5. **Prototype now:** an evaluator-dependency graph attached to each evaluation run, with source, suite, provider, infrastructure, and model-family dimensions.
6. **Evidence of progress:** correlated evaluator groups are correctly capped; independent groups catch seeded blind spots; disagreement records predict future evaluator failures better than simple model counts.
7. **Failure modes:** undisclosed shared data, shell evaluator organizations, benchmark leakage, and shared prompt/rubric weaknesses can create false diversity.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** EvidenceIndependence, EvaluatorIndependenceProfile, EvaluatorDisagreement, IndependentAIEvaluator, DisagreementCap.



## Trustworthiness score gaming

1. **Why it matters:** once ratings affect deployment, providers and systems can optimize for rating appearance rather than safer behavior.
2. **What is already known:** hard caps, hidden suites, immutable history, independent evidence, and no-averaging critical rules reduce some gaming paths.
3. **What remains unknown:** which scoring policies remain robust under adaptive optimization, benchmark leakage, favorable scoping, and selective evidence publication.
4. **Candidate research directions:** adversarial scoring-policy tournaments, metric-gaming red teams, hidden-suite rotation, production/evaluation drift analysis, and incentives for adverse-evidence disclosure.
5. **Prototype now:** a rating-cap calculator with adversarial fixtures for denominator inflation, stale evidence, evaluator correlation, unresolved reports, and version laundering.
6. **Evidence of progress:** gaming fixtures reduce or suspend ratings as intended; providers cannot improve rating by hiding failures, narrowing scope silently, or relabeling N/A.
7. **Failure modes:** overly rigid anti-gaming rules may punish real improvements, while overly flexible rules may be captured by provider narratives.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** TrustworthinessScoringPolicy, CriticalErrorCap, FreshnessCap, IndependenceCap, DisagreementCap, ChangeCarryoverCap.



## Privacy-preserving public error evidence

1. **Why it matters:** public error evidence supports accountability, but raw prompts, outputs, telemetry, or exploit detail may expose private people or unsafe instructions.
2. **What is already known:** privacy tiers, minimization, redaction, provenance, and evidence references can separate public claims from restricted source evidence.
3. **What remains unknown:** how to make public summaries independently checkable without leaking sensitive data, enabling attacks, or letting providers hide decisive context.
4. **Candidate research directions:** verifiable redaction, selective disclosure, zero-knowledge or commitment-based evidence summaries, differential privacy for aggregate error rates, and trusted reviewer access models.
5. **Prototype now:** a public/private AI Error Registry split with redacted public records, restricted evidence packages, audit hashes, and reviewer challenge workflow.
6. **Evidence of progress:** reviewers can verify disposition and rating effect while privacy attacks and exploit reconstruction fail on public artifacts.
7. **Failure modes:** redaction may remove the facts needed for review, privacy mechanisms may hide supplier misconduct, or aggregate releases may leak membership.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AIErrorRegistry, EvidenceCarryingAlertProtocol, StructuredEvidencePackage, RatingExplanationRecord, PrivacyPreservationScore.



## Evaluator recursion and trust roots

1. **Why it matters:** AI evaluators can scale review, but "AI evaluates AI" becomes circular if evaluator trust is not anchored in independent evidence and governance.
2. **What is already known:** evaluator identity, scenario suites, rubrics, independence profiles, and retained disagreement are necessary but not enough.
3. **What remains unknown:** where the trust chain must terminate, how to evaluate evaluator drift, and when human/lab review remains mandatory.
4. **Candidate research directions:** evaluator assurance cases, recursive-evaluator failure tests, trust-root registries, spot-check economics, and domain rules for human/lab override.
5. **Prototype now:** an evaluator registry linking each AI evaluator to its own evidence profile, allowed domains, independence claims, review expiry, and mandatory human/lab sampling rates.
6. **Evidence of progress:** recursive chains remain finite and inspectable; evaluator drift is detected; critical verdicts trigger non-recursive review where required.
7. **Failure modes:** evaluator chains may share hidden roots, trust may be delegated indefinitely, or human sampling may become symbolic rather than evidential.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** IndependentAIEvaluator, EvaluatorIndependenceProfile, AITrustworthinessEvaluationProtocol, AITrustworthinessRegistry, GuardianDiversityScore.



## Cross-domain trustworthiness calibration

1. **Why it matters:** the same rating label can imply very different evidence strength and consequence risk across medicine, finance, robotics, education, and general conversation.
2. **What is already known:** UAIS requires domain-scoped profiles and forbids automatic transfer across domains, versions, configurations, or environments.
3. **What remains unknown:** whether any limited cross-domain calibration is valid, and how to communicate non-comparability without making ratings unusable.
4. **Candidate research directions:** domain bridge studies, consequence-normalized uncertainty communication, rating-label comprehension tests, cross-domain failure taxonomies, and procurement misuse studies.
5. **Prototype now:** a cross-domain comparison report that shows what can and cannot transfer, lists missing strata, and requires explicit bridge evidence before comparison.
6. **Evidence of progress:** users stop treating equal labels as equal risk; bridge mappings predict held-out cross-domain failures; procurement clauses preserve domain scope.
7. **Failure modes:** bridge studies may hide incommensurable harms, oversimplified labels may create false equivalence, and market pressure may push toward one misleading universal score.
8. **UAIS dependencies:** AITrustworthinessProfile, TrustworthinessRatingLevel, TrustworthinessScoringPolicy, TrustworthinessCarryoverRule, ConsumerAISafetyClass.
