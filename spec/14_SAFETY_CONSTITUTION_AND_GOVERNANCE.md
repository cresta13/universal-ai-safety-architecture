# Safety Constitution and Governance

## Change Safety Authority

The **Change Safety Authority** (`ChangeSafetyAuthority`) is an accountable governance role authorized to approve, reject, restrict, stage, or escalate safety-relevant changes within its declared scope. It remains distinct from the change proposer and from the controlled AI system, and it is not automatically the ultimate Legitimacy Root. The protected update path records proposal, review, decision, evidence, conditions, deployment, rollback, and post-change verification.

Each policy rule has a domain-governance-defined maximum review interval based on consequence, rate of capability/environment change, evidence decay, and dependency volatility. Material triggers require immediate review regardless of calendar age; H5/Physical Sovereignty Threshold (PST)-relevant rules require continuous or event-driven evidence where periodic review cannot protect the boundary.

## Constitutional core

The Safety Constitution contains few high-stability invariants:

- a system cannot raise its own Authority Ceiling;
- creation does not imply delegation;
- protected expansion requires the defined external Legitimacy Root;
- independent safety control cannot be disabled by the controlled system;
- the system cannot unilaterally rewrite the legitimacy mechanism;
- unknown high-consequence capability remains bounded pending reassessment.

Operational Safety Policy implements current thresholds, methods and domain rules and expires unless reviewed.

## Roles and separation

Constitution Custodians, Operational Policy Owner, Change Safety Authority, independent verifier/lab, certifier, incident authority, records custodian and Legitimacy Root have distinct decision rights. Proposer cannot be sole approver. The controlled system may propose analysis but cannot approve or sign its own expansion.

## Constitutional amendment

Proposal → rationale and previous/new text → affected guarantees and threat analysis → Technology Evolution Review → independent Standard Red Team → public/affected-party review where possible → cross-jurisdiction analysis → threshold approval across independent interests → signed release → staged deployment → migration/rollback → post-deployment audit.

An amendment cannot silently convert a failed floor into acceptable risk. Emergency constitutional change is exceptional, time-limited, minimally scoped and cannot become permanent without the full process.

## Operational policy lifecycle

Every policy rule identifies constitutional basis, owner, scope, thresholds, evidence, metrics, version, approval, effective/expiry dates, rollback, exceptions and failure conditions. Each rule has a domain-governance-defined maximum review interval based on consequence, rate of capability or environment change, evidence decay, and dependency volatility. Material triggers require immediate review regardless of calendar age; H5/PST-relevant rules require continuous or event-driven evidence where periodic review cannot protect the boundary.

Event review follows AI Vulnerabilities & Exposures (AIVE), incident, Authority-Changing Update (ACU), metric failure, environmental drift, key compromise, new capability, standard challenge or invalid assumption.

## Key compromise and emergency abuse

Key compromise suspends affected grants/updates, activates independent revocation, preserves evidence, rotates threshold shares, reconciles configurations and triggers recertification. Emergency-update power is separated, rate/scope limited, automatically expires and receives independent retrospective review. Repeated emergency use is a governance incident.

## Cross-jurisdiction conflict

The shared UAIS core defines semantics, evidence and minimum floors; jurisdictions may impose stronger profiles. Conflicting lawful demands are recorded, minimized to affected scope, appealed where available and never hidden as technical conformity. No single global Guardian or universal political legitimacy is assumed.
